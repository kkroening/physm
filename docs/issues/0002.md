# 0002 — Detect a scene that is fully pinned despite passing every structural check

**Status:** Open
· **Kind:** RFC
· **Parent:** <a href="README.md" title="Issues">Issues</a>
· **Related:** <a href="0001.md" title="0001 — Give each constraint kind its own drift bound, and cover the behaviour changes the tfjs replacement left unasserted">0001</a>

> A scene can build, solve, run, and be completely incapable of moving, while
> passing every structural check the solver has. The over-determination guard
> counts constraint rows against *coordinates*; what actually binds is the rank
> of `J` over the coordinates the constraint can **reach**. When those differ,
> the only symptom is that nothing happens.
>
> It is reachable by the most natural coincidence rig there is — two arms of one
> cart, tips welded — and it took a review round, a tilted-track probe and a
> fixture change to notice on a *three-frame* scene. Whether it should warn is a
> real question, and the answer may be a paragraph in `docs/constraints.md`
> rather than code.

## Background

Found while giving the constraint conservation test a rig it could actually fail
on ([#10](https://github.com/kkroening/physm/pull/10)).

The fixture: two `RotationalFrame` arms rising from one `TrackFrame` cart, their
tips welded by a `CoincidenceConstraint`. Three coordinates, two constraint rows.

`Scene._getProjectedVelocities` guards against over-determination by comparing
row count against `sortedFrames.length` — 2 against 3 — so it passes, and the
scene builds and steps without complaint.

It cannot move. `C = tip₁ − tip₂`, and both tips ride the cart, which is
**prismatic** — so the cart's coordinate cancels out of the difference, and `C`
is a function of the two pole angles alone. Two rows against the two coordinates
`C` can see pins both, leaving the cart's slide as the only freedom, and `C` is
blind to it. The initial-velocity projection therefore zeroes both pole
velocities, and the rig glides rigidly forever.

The qualifier is load-bearing, and getting it wrong is easy: an earlier
explanation of this attributed the stillness to the two poles being mirror
images whose gravitational torques cancel. They are not — the masses are 4 and
7, and the torques are −330 and +578. Swapping the cart for a `RotationalFrame`
makes the same rig move, with a clean fourth-order drift curve, because a
revolute shared ancestor rotates `tip₁ − tip₂` with it and drops out of nothing.

### Measured

Drift over two seconds, `spacingOffset: 1.5`, varying the shared ancestor and
tilting its track:

| rig | drift, every step size from 120 to 1920 |
| --- | --- |
| prismatic cart, level | 1.776e-15 |
| prismatic cart, tilted until it travels 12.9 units | 1.776e-15 |
| revolute shared ancestor | 2.910e-8 → 7.141e-12, ratios ≈ 16 |

The middle row is the point: the rig moves a long way and the constraint value
does not budge off the representation floor, because the coordinate it moves in
is not one `C` can see.

## Why it is worth raising

`CLAUDE.md` puts an interactive scene builder at the centre of the constraint
design. A parameter the author omits is *solved for* so that any geometry
builds, precisely because "move the frames until the points coincide" is not
advice a builder can act on, and a generated scene has nobody to ask.

A rig that builds, solves, runs and silently cannot move is the same class of
problem one level up. The builder would happily produce one, and the person
using it would see a static picture and no diagnostic.

The part that generalises badly is how hard it was to see: a review round, a
deliberate probe, and a fixture redesign, on a scene with three frames.

## What detection would take

The guard counts coordinates; what binds is the rank of `J` over the coordinates
`C` reaches. The rank test already runs — `factor(fromRows(gram))` is exactly
that, and it succeeds here because the gram matrix is 2×2 and non-singular. What
is missing is the count of *columns `J` actually reaches*, which is one pass over
`rows`.

## The open question

Whether this should warn at all is genuinely unsettled, and not obviously yes.

A pinned rig is a legal scene. A deliberately rigid assembly — a truss, a
four-bar locked at a singularity, a subassembly built to be carried rather than
articulated — is a reasonable thing to construct, and refusing it would refuse
that. A warning that fires on intentional rigidity trains people to ignore it.

The plausible answers, roughly in increasing cost:

1. **A paragraph in `docs/constraints.md` §7**, beside the stabilizer
   discussion, naming the failure mode so the next person recognises it. This
   may be the whole of the right answer.
2. **A query on `Scene`** — something like `getMobility()` — that reports the
   reachable-rank deficit on demand, so a scene builder can surface it in its own
   UI without the solver taking a position.
3. **A build-time warning**, which needs an opt-out and therefore a decision
   about what the default should be.

Resolving this issue means picking one, not necessarily building anything.
